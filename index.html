I
Introduction & Icebreaker
Set the tone. Let them show you who they are before the real questions start.
Before you start
This candidate is a sales professional. They are comfortable performing. They will be warm, confident, and good at first impressions — that is their job. Don't let a strong opening fool you. Use this section to set the expectation that this interview goes deeper than most.
1 Your intro & opening
"Hey ___, welcome — I'm ___, I'm one of the recruiters here at Proliance. Before I get into the formal stuff, I want to give you a heads up: this is a different kind of interview. We're going to get pretty specific — I'll be asking for real numbers, real situations, real names. Not because we're trying to trip you up, but because this role demands that level and we want to make sure we're not wasting each other's time. Sound good?"
Why open this way
You are pre-framing the entire interview. A strong candidate will lean in. A weak candidate will get slightly nervous — and that tension will surface in their answers. You've also removed their ability to give vague answers later without looking like they ignored your framing.
✓ Strong reaction
Smiles, says something like "I appreciate that" — confident
Leans forward — they're ready for it
Asks a clarifying question about the role — already engaged
✗ Watch for
Nervous laugh or visible shift — they came prepared to perform, not to be real
Immediately starts pitching themselves before you've asked anything
Overconfident dismissal — "oh I've done plenty of these"
2 First real question
"So ___ — in one sentence, not a paragraph: what are you best at, professionally?"
Why one sentence
You're testing self-awareness and precision. A closer who can't distill their own value proposition into one sentence will struggle to do it for a homeowner. Watch whether they actually honor the constraint — or immediately expand into a paragraph anyway.
✓ Strong
Crisp, specific — "I close difficult deals other people have already walked away from"
Answers in one sentence and stops — self-discipline
The sentence is about results, not personality traits
✗ Watch for
Goes on for 3–4 sentences — can't edit themselves
Personality-based — "I'm a people person, I'm very driven..."
Generic — "I'm good at sales and building relationships"
Notes
Self-aware, precise, result-oriented
II
Production Track Record
Real numbers. No ranges. No approximations.
Context for you
This is the section most candidates prepare for — and still fail. They'll give you ranges, impressive-sounding stories, and context about why their numbers weren't higher. Push every time for the exact number. "Around 85%" is not the same as 85%. If they can't give you specifics on their own performance, they will not be able to hold their team to specifics either.
1 Closing numbers must-have deal-breaker
"___, what was your personal closing percentage in your last role — not a range, the actual number? And what was the average deal size?"
What you're actually testing
Specificity is the filter. Someone who tracked their numbers knows them exactly. Someone who didn't track them — or is inflating — will give you a range or hedge. The follow-up is what breaks them: ask what their worst month looked like. Strong performers know their lows as well as their highs.
✓ Strong
Gives an exact number — "87.3%" not "around 85–90"
Can also tell you their worst month without being asked
Knows average deal size immediately
✗ Red flags
Gives a range — "somewhere between 80 and 90 percent"
Qualifies immediately — "well it depends on the market..."
Can't remember deal size — means they weren't tracking
If they give a range — follow-up
"I need the actual number, not a range — what does your CRM or tracker show?"
"What was your closing % in your worst quarter last year specifically?"
Exit speech — if they can't give real numbers
"___, I want to be straight with you — for this role, we need someone who tracks their performance at a very specific level, because they'll be accountable to specific numbers every single week. The fact that you don't have those figures ready tells me we might not be aligned on what that level of accountability looks like. I don't want to waste your time moving forward if that's the case."
Notes
Gave exact numbers — no hedging
2 Hardest deal they ever closed jolt
"Tell me about the hardest deal you ever closed. Not your biggest — the hardest. What made it hard, and what specifically did you do that got it over the line?"
What you're actually testing
Easy deals tell you nothing. Hard deals reveal technique. You're listening for whether they understand what was actually hard — the objection, the client psychology, the situation — and whether their solution was strategic or just lucky. Vague answers here mean they've never truly analyzed their own wins.
✓ Strong
Names the specific obstacle — not just "it was a tough client"
Describes what they did differently — not just persistence
Can tell you what they learned from it
✗ Red flags
"I just kept following up" — persistence without strategy
The hard part was logistics, not the actual close
Vague on what they actually said or did to turn it
If their answer is vague — follow-up
"What exactly did you say when they pushed back? Walk me through the actual conversation."
"At what point did you know you had them? What shifted?"
Notes
Strategic closer — not just persistent
3 A deal they lost jolt
"Now tell me about a deal you lost that you should have closed. What actually happened?"
What you're actually testing
This is an accountability trap disguised as a question. Watch whether they own the loss or explain it. Strong closers know exactly what they did wrong. Weak closers blame the client, the market, the product. The word "but" after "I lost it because..." is a red flag every time.
✓ Strong
Owns it immediately — names their specific mistake
Can tell you what they would do differently today
No external blame in the story
✗ Red flags
The loss was primarily the client's fault — "they just weren't ready"
External factors dominate the story — market, price, competition
Can't think of one — means they've never honestly reviewed a loss
Notes
Owns losses — no blame language
III
Coaching & Development
Can they teach what they do — or do they just do it?
Context for you
The most common failure in player-coach roles: elite individual performers who cannot articulate why they're good. They close by instinct and can't break it down for someone else. Ask them to teach you something — not just tell you about it. That gap is where most candidates fall apart.
1 Coaching track record must-have
"___, tell me about someone you coached who genuinely improved because of you. Not a team win — one specific person. What was their closing % when you started working with them, what was it when you were done, and what did you actually do to get them there?"
What you're actually testing
You want a name, a before number, an after number, and a specific technique. "I helped my team improve" is not an answer. If they can't give you one specific person with real data, they have not actually coached — they've managed. Those are not the same thing.
✓ Strong
Names the person (first name is fine)
Gives before and after numbers
Describes the specific technique or change that made the difference
✗ Red flags
Team-level answer — no individual named
No numbers — just "they really improved"
Their coaching was mostly motivation, not technique
If their answer is vague — follow-up
"What was their actual closing % before and after?"
"Walk me through a specific session — what did you say to them?"
Exit speech — if they can't name one person with real data
"___, I appreciate the answer — but I need to be honest. This role is 25% coaching and that coaching has to produce measurable results on two teams. If you can't point to a specific person whose numbers moved because of something you specifically did, that's a gap I can't overlook for this position."
Notes
Real coaching results — named, numbered
2 Player-coach tension jolt
"Here's a real scenario, ___: it's Wednesday. You have three personal appointments today and your highest-potential closer just called — they're in a deal right now and they're stuck, they need you live. What do you do?"
What you're actually testing
This is the core tension of the player-coach role. There is no perfect answer — and that's the point. You're watching how they think through competing priorities in real time. Do they protect their own production? Do they drop everything for the team? Do they have a framework — or do they just wing it?
✓ Strong
Thinks out loud — doesn't give an instant perfect answer
Has a system for triage — knows what to protect vs. what to sacrifice
Acknowledges the tension honestly — "this is the hard part of the role"
✗ Red flags
Instant "I'd always prioritize the team" — no real thought, sounds rehearsed
Instant "I'd reschedule the team member" — shows they haven't thought about team development
Can't articulate a framework at all — will figure it out as they go
Notes
Has a framework for production vs. team tension
IV
Handling Underperformers
Do they have the stomach for it — or will they protect people too long?
Context for you
This is where most player-coaches fail silently. They keep underperformers too long because removing them means carrying more personal production. The candidate who sounds compassionate in their answer may be the one who lets the team rot for six months before doing anything. Listen for whether they can hold the standard while still being human about it.
1 The hypothetical must-have deal-breaker
"___, here's a situation I want your instinct on. One of your top closers — someone the team genuinely likes, been there 18 months, you've invested real time in them — has been at 71% closing for six straight weeks. They've got a family situation going on. What do you do, and when do you do it?"
Reading their answer — what to watch for
Watch how fast they answer AND what they say. Instant answer = they've thought about this OR they're giving you the "right" answer without real feeling. Long pause = they're actually thinking it through — that's better. The word "but" is the tell: "I'd have the conversation but given the family situation..." — that "but" means they'd delay. The standard doesn't move for circumstances. A strong candidate knows that and says it.
✓ Strong instinct
Has the direct conversation immediately — week 1, not week 6
Separates the personal situation from the performance standard clearly
Has a defined timeline with a specific consequence — not open-ended
Shows care for the person without lowering the bar
✗ Red flags
"Given the family situation, I'd give them more time" — the circumstance moved the standard
No defined timeline — "I'd work with them and see how it goes"
Talks about the relationship more than the performance
Can't say the word "remove" — they'll never do it in time
Push harder — follow-up questions
"At what exact week do you make the removal decision — if they haven't improved?"
"Have you ever actually removed someone you liked? What happened?"
Exit speech — if they can't hold the standard
"___, I hear the compassion in that — and it says something good about you as a person. But I have to be honest: what I'm hearing is that the circumstance would move the standard. In this role, the standard doesn't move. If I can't trust that the bar gets held regardless of who the person is or what's going on in their life, this role isn't the right fit — for either of us."
Notes
Holds the standard — clear timeline, no excuses
2 Have they actually done it jolt
"Have you ever had to let someone go that you genuinely liked? Tell me what happened — not the policy, the actual conversation."
What you're actually testing
Hypotheticals are easy. You want evidence they've done the hard thing in real life. The detail in their story — how the conversation opened, what the person said, how they felt afterward — tells you whether this is lived experience or theory.
✓ Strong
Tells a specific story — real name (first name), real situation
Describes the actual conversation, not just the decision
Shows it was hard but necessary — not casual about it
✗ Red flags
Can't think of one — has never done it or avoids it
Story is about HR making the decision — they didn't actually do it
Casual or performatively tough — "I don't have a problem with it"
Notes
Has done it — real story, real conversation
V
Pressure & Accountability
Do they own the number — or explain it?
Context for you
Sales leaders are exceptionally good at contextualizing poor performance. Market conditions, insurance complexity, team turnover — there's always a reason. You need someone who hears the number and says "I own that" — not "here's why it looks that way." The framing of their answer tells you everything.
1 A period of underperformance jolt
"Tell me about a stretch — at least a month — where your numbers were below where they needed to be. What caused it, and what did you do?"
What you're actually testing
Listen to the ratio of external explanation vs. internal ownership. Strong answer: "My pipeline management broke down, I wasn't qualifying leads properly, I fixed it by..." Weak answer: "The market was slow, we had some bad leads, eventually things picked back up." Both can be true — which one they lead with is who they are.
✓ Strong
Owns the slump without being asked to
Identifies what specifically broke down in their process
Describes a concrete change they made — not just trying harder
✗ Red flags
External factors dominate — market, leads, management
Recovery was passive — "things picked back up"
Can't remember a stretch of underperformance — never had one, or won't admit it
Notes
Owns the slump — identifies specific fix
2 Accountability to two numbers simultaneously must-have
"In this role you're accountable to two numbers at once — your personal closing % and your team's closing %. If your personal numbers are strong but your team is struggling, how do you feel about that — and what do you do?"
What you're actually testing
This question reveals whether they see leadership as part of their identity or a bonus responsibility. A true player-coach feels genuine discomfort when the team struggles even if they personally are performing. Someone who's really just a closer with a title will feel fine about it.
✓ Strong
Immediate discomfort — "that's not okay, that's on me"
Sees team performance as a reflection of their coaching quality
Has a specific response — pulls back on personal appointments to focus on team
✗ Red flags
Comfortable with it — "well at least I'm carrying my weight"
Puts team performance on the individuals — "they need to want it"
No clear action — "I'd talk to them about it"
Notes
Owns team performance as their own responsibility
VI
Culture & Leadership Style
Do they lead from the front — or manage from behind?
What Proliance culture actually looks like
Lead from the frontNon-negotiable standardsBuild leaders not followersUrgency & disciplineHigh personal accountabilitySlow to hire, fast to remove
Context for you
The JD says "you do not manage from the sidelines." This isn't just a phrase — it's a cultural filter. Leaders who prefer strategy over execution, who delegate before they demonstrate, who build process before they build credibility — they don't survive here. You need someone who earns respect by outperforming.
1 Leadership philosophy jolt
"___, what's the worst leadership habit you've seen in a sales manager — the thing that makes a team worse, not better?"
What you're listening for
What they can't stand in others reveals who they are. If they resent managers who don't produce — they produce. If they hate managers who play favorites — they hold standards. If they complain about micromanagement — watch out, they may resist accountability themselves.
✓ Culture match
Hates managers who don't lead by example — "how can you coach it if you don't do it"
Frustrated by managers who protect underperformers
Bothered by vague feedback — "good job, keep it up"
✗ Culture mismatch
Hates managers who are "too numbers-focused" — they'll resist accountability
Complains about being held to strict standards — won't hold others to them
Vague answer — "just bad communication" with no specifics
Notes
Leadership instincts align with our culture
2 Weekend availability must-have
"This role runs appointments on Saturdays and Sundays from 9 to 3 — homeowners aren't available during office hours during the week, so we go to them. That's non-negotiable. What does your weekend currently look like and what would you be giving up?"
Why ask it this way
Same principle as the Field Runner script — "what would you be giving up" surfaces real conflicts before they become reliability problems three months in. A confident candidate has already thought about this.
✓ Strong
Says yes without conditions
Has worked weekend closing schedules before — not new to them
✗ Disqualifier
"Most weekends should be fine" — that's a no
Has a recurring conflict — church commitments, second job, family obligation
Notes
Weekend availability confirmed — no conditions
VII
Roleplay — Objection Handling
You play the homeowner. Watch their instinct, calm, and technique.
How to run this section
You are the homeowner. Read each line out loud, stay in character. After they respond, use the reading guide to assess. You can improvise — go off script, push back harder, see how they handle an unexpected response. The edit icon on each homeowner line means you can reword it live.
SetupContext for the candidate before you begin
"___, I want to do a quick roleplay. I'm going to be a homeowner — you're closing me on a roofing upgrade. The insurance is covering the basic replacement. You're going to try to move me to a premium upgrade. I'm going to give you some real objections we hear in the field. Just respond the way you would live. Ready?"
1 The insurance objection roleplay

Read this to them — you are the homeowner:

"Look, the insurance is already paying for the roof. I just want what's covered — I don't want to pay anything out of pocket."
↑ You can click and edit this line to improvise
✓ Strong response
Acknowledges the insurance coverage first — doesn't fight it
Reframes as a timing window: "you're already doing the replacement — this is the lowest cost moment to upgrade"
Makes it about the homeowner's long-term interest, not the sale
Simple, clear — doesn't over-explain product specs
✗ Weak response
Immediately goes into product features — lost the emotional moment
Makes the homeowner feel upsold — "well if you just add a little more..."
Over-complicated explanation of insurance coverage
Pressure close — "you really should consider this"
The ideal response sounds like...
"Totally understand — and the insurance does cover the replacement, that's covered. What I want to show you is: since your roof is already coming off and going back on, the labor cost is already there. Adding a premium product right now costs a fraction of what it would cost as a standalone project in five years. This is the one window where upgrading makes financial sense. Can I show you the difference in cost?"
Notes
Reframes timing — keeps it simple and client-focused
2 The money objection roleplay

Read this to them:

"I hear you, but honestly — I just don't have the money for upgrades right now."
✓ Strong response
Slows down — doesn't panic or immediately discount
Asks a question: "when you say you don't have the money, are you thinking cash or...?"
Explores financing without pushing it
Finds out if it's a real budget constraint or a value objection in disguise
✗ Weak response
Accepts it and moves on — lost the deal without a fight
Immediately drops the upgrade — "okay, we can just do basic"
Pushes financing before understanding the real objection
The ideal response sounds like...
"I hear you — and I don't want to put you in a position that doesn't make sense. Can I ask — when you say you don't have the money, are you thinking about this as a cash expense right now? Because we do have financing options that spread this out, and a lot of homeowners find the monthly difference is smaller than they expected. Would that be worth looking at for a second?"
Notes
Asks, doesn't assume — explores before accepting
3 The selling-the-house objection roleplay

Read this to them:

"We're actually planning to sell the house in the next year or two. Now's not the time to invest in upgrades — I'll let the new owners deal with it."
✓ Strong response
Flips the frame immediately: upgrades increase listing value and differentiate the home
Makes it about the seller's return, not the product
"A new roof with premium materials is a selling point — basic coverage is the minimum buyers expect"
✗ Weak response
Agrees and drops the upgrade entirely
Pushes back on the selling plan — "are you sure you're selling?"
Can't make the ROI argument for upgrades before a sale
The ideal response sounds like...
"Actually, if you're selling — this matters even more. Buyers are going to inspect that roof and the quality of the materials will show up in the report. A premium roof is a selling point you can market. Basic coverage is the floor — everyone expects it. You're already replacing the roof; the upgrade cost is marginal compared to what it does for your listing price and buyer confidence."
Notes
Flips to listing value — makes the ROI case
4 The spouse objection roleplay

Read this to them:

"This sounds interesting but I can't decide without talking to my husband first. He handles the finances."
✓ Strong response
Does NOT push to close right now — respects the process
Sets a specific next step: "Absolutely — can we get a time this week when you're both available?"
Offers to prepare materials for the conversation with the spouse
Keeps the momentum without forcing a decision
✗ Weak response
Tries to close right now anyway — creates resentment and loses the deal
Leaves it completely open — "okay just let me know"
No next step — deal dies in the follow-up black hole
The ideal response sounds like...
"Of course — this is a joint decision and I wouldn't want you to feel rushed. Can we find a time this week, even just 20 minutes, when you're both available? I'd love to walk through it together so he has the same information you have and any questions get answered. Would Thursday evening or Saturday morning work?"
Notes
Sets specific next step — doesn't push or abandon
5 The stall objection roleplay

Read this to them:

"I just need a little more time to think about it. I'll let you know."
✓ Strong response
Doesn't accept the vague close — "I completely understand — what specifically is making you hesitate?"
Identifies the real underlying concern before agreeing to follow up
Gets a specific callback time — not just "I'll be in touch"
✗ Weak response
"Sure, take your time" — the deal is dead and they don't know it
Pushes harder without understanding the real hesitation
No effort to surface the actual objection
The ideal response sounds like...
"Absolutely, I never want you to feel rushed. Can I ask — is there a specific part of this you're not sure about? Because sometimes what feels like 'I need to think' is actually a specific question that hasn't been answered yet, and I'd rather answer it now than leave you with something unresolved. What's on your mind?"
Notes
Surfaces the real objection — doesn't accept the stall
6 The competitor objection roleplay

Read this to them:

"I want to get a few more quotes. And actually — I have a friend who does roofing, so I'm probably going to go with him."
✓ Strong response
Doesn't panic or go negative about the friend
Raises the insurance claim risk — "working with an unlicensed contractor on an insurance job can void the claim"
Competes on reliability, warranty, and the insurance process — not price
Stays warm — "I think that's a fair thing to explore"
✗ Weak response
Speaks negatively about the friend or competition
Immediately tries to match price or discount
Gets defensive or aggressive
Accepts the loss without addressing the trust issue
The ideal response sounds like...
"I think that's completely fair — I'd never tell you not to get other quotes. One thing I do want to flag: when you're dealing with an insurance claim, the contractor needs to be licensed and bonded to work within the claim process. Using someone outside of that can create complications with your adjuster or void portions of the claim. I'd just ask you to verify that your friend is set up for insurance work before you commit. Assuming they are — I'd still love the chance to show you what we bring to it."
Notes
Competes on trust and risk — not price, not negativity

Closing & Compensation
The gate conditions will filter for you. Watch the reaction.
Context for you
The comp structure is intentionally performance-gated. The override is only unlocked if the team hits 83% closing AND 15% upgrade. A strong candidate hears this and immediately thinks about how to get the team there. A weak candidate immediately asks what happens if the gate isn't hit. That first question tells you everything about their confidence in their own ability to lead.

1 Comp delivery deal-breaker
"Here's the comp structure, ___. Base is $70K. Your personal production earns you 5% on retail and upgrades, 2.5% on ACV. The team override — which is where your highest earning potential sits — is gated. You only unlock it when the team hits 83% closing AND 15% upgrade. If either misses, no override that period. Total target year one is $95–125K. What's your first question?"
What their first question tells you
Strong candidate: "Where is the team currently at on those numbers?" — they're already thinking about the gap they need to close. Weak candidate: "What happens if we don't hit the gate?" — they're already planning for failure. The first question is the most revealing thing they'll say in this section.
✓ Strong reaction
"Where is the team currently?" — thinking about the path to unlock
Confident about their ability to hit the gate — "that's achievable"
Asks about team structure, deal flow, or support — already thinking operationally
✗ Red flags
"What if the gate isn't hit?" — first question is about failure
Tries to negotiate the base before proving anything
Visible drop in energy — the gate scared them
Exit speech — if they negotiate before they've earned anything
"___, I hear you on the base — and I want to be transparent. The structure is designed so that the highest earning potential comes from team performance, not negotiating the floor. If the first instinct is to negotiate the base before you've produced a single result, that tells me something about where your confidence sits in your ability to earn through performance. That's the conversation I'd want us to have."
Notes
First question is about winning — not about failing
